Beyond Research

Chinese Calligraphy

I am a professional artist specializing in Chinese calligraphy. Long before I began working with data and models, I learned to think through the movement of a brush. I began practicing Chinese calligraphy in childhood and later studied under Nie Chengwen, a distinguished contemporary calligrapher who served for three consecutive terms as vice chairman of the China Calligraphers Association and now serves as an advisor to the Association. My training spans clerical, cursive, regular, running, and seal scripts, yet my artistic language is grounded most deeply in clerical script. I am drawn to its broad, weighty forms and monumental strength. Combined with a distinctly northern Chinese temperament—direct, open, and unrestrained—it has shaped a style that is grounded in structure yet expansive in scale and spirit. These qualities taught me to see discipline and expression not as opposites, but as forces that give each other form. Over time, I joined the Liaoning Calligraphers Association and published Xianjia Gao Calligraphy Collection. Calligraphy remains a living artistic practice—one that continues to shape how I observe, compose, and make decisions.

Why Wang Xizhi (王羲之)

Of all classical calligraphers, Wang Xizhi is the artist I return to most often. I admire not only the elegance of his line, but also the intelligence of his balance: restraint without stiffness, variation without disorder, and movement held together by structure.

His words from Lantingji Xu capture what I value in both art and inquiry—the ability to hold the whole in view while remaining attentive to the richness of each particular.

The brush and the model

From a single stroke to an entire system

“一点成一字之规，一字乃终篇之准。” “A single dot sets the standard for a character; a single character, for the entire composition.” Sun Guoting, Shu Pu

This idea stays with me in research. In calligraphy, a single stroke must serve both the character and the composition. In my work, a local operational decision—how a food item is allocated, how a pantry sets a point value, or how a pharmacy replenishes an essential medicine—can reshape waste, access, and equity across a larger system.

Calligraphy and research therefore ask a similar question: how can individual choices be coordinated into a whole that is rigorous, responsive, and humane? One works through brush and ink; the other through data, optimization, machine learning, and field experiments. Both begin with close observation and end with a design for the whole.

A distinctive week in Beijing

Two solo exhibitions, five days apart

In May 2016, I presented two solo exhibitions in Beijing. Spring Lines opened at the University of International Business and Economics on May 10; the university described it as its first solo exhibition by a current student in recent years. Xian · Ink opened five days later at Shengshi Tiankong Art Museum in the 798 Art District. At the first exhibition, I donated two works to the university library and its Department of Culture and Arts.
